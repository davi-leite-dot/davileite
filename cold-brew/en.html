    Mind your caffeine curve

    The curve changes with the time you drink and with the amount. After you drink, caffeine rises in the blood, peaks in about 45 minutes and is cleared gradually: roughly every 5 hours it halves. The light band shows the range between fast and slow metabolisers.

    What's left at bedtime

    Even if you don't feel it, caffeine still circulating at night can delay sleep and make it lighter. The earlier and smaller the dose, the cleaner the night.

    Suggested equipment

    Three paths, from the simplest to the most professional. The method matters more than the vessel.

    The starting point

    Any vessel

    Any vessel that holds the cold brew works for brewing and storing: from a PET bottle to an aluminium milk pan, a large glass or a blender jar. Infuse it any way you like.

    • What matters is the method, not really the vessel.
    • How you filter afterwards affects the result.
    • How the coffee settles and its total contact area with the water also play a part.
    • But what really matters is making the cold brew.
    Intermediate · used in the video

    Glass jar with an airtight lid and a fine-mesh filter basket that attaches to the lid.

    • With the basket: put the ground coffee inside the basket and the final filtering is done. In the recipe I did it differently, with loose coffee.
    • With loose coffee: steep everything loose in the jar and, at the end, filter through a V60 or a Melitta-style paper filter.

    My favourite

    Toddy Cold Brew System

    The most professional and most recommended way to make cold brew. A brewing container of about 2 litres, with a reusable felt filter and a silicone stopper at the bottom, set over a glass decanter.

    • The coffee steeps in the container with the stopper in.
    • At the end, just pull the stopper: the cold brew drains, filtered, into the glass decanter, which goes straight into the fridge.
    See the Toddy on AliExpress

    Storage

    All the extracted cold brew, once filtered, can be kept in the fridge in a closed container and lasts up to 5 days. Pour your glasses from it.

    Tip: use mineral water for brewing.

    Out of the fridge: worth a try

    If the cold brew is left out of the fridge or warms up a little, very curious aromas and notes appear: a sweeter, more caramelised smell and interesting volatiles. One way to drink it is to keep it warm and add the ice only when serving, mixing that aroma with the cold freshness. In that case, shelf life drops from 5 to 2 or 3 days.

    Author's notes

    What I have learned making, drinking and studying this coffee.

    A pure cold brew, with nothing added, is a wide, simple, very rich and harmonious spectrum. It is a very peculiar and very valuable sensory experience.

    An almost personal recipe

    Of everything out there about cold brew, this recipe is almost entirely personal. It carries my own traits: my knowledge, reading, research and history with the coffee world. If you like it, build your own trail. A lot is being done in drinks and pairings: with orange, with ice, with tonic and many other recipes, across a very wide range of possibilities.

    Coffee from Monday to Friday

    One advantage I took into my routine: I don't have to brew coffee all the time for coffee to be worth it. No cables, no heating water, no going back for more. I make it on Monday and have coffee until Friday, cold, tasty and ready. It brings planning and ease.

    Coffee satiety, with a catch

    Cold brew gives you coffee satiety. The catch is how hard it is to drink just a little: you want litres of it, at any time. Keep the caffeine in mind.

    Why it is hard to find in cafés

    Many specialty cafés struggle to keep this coffee fresh and meet demand. On a hot day, if ten people order it, the stock is gone; with consumption still small, there is almost no predictability. So if a cold brew like this costs 30, 40 or 50 reais, it is still cheap given the waste and how hard it is to forecast.

    Pairings

    It goes very well with crumbly butter biscuits, with cinnamon and other spiced biscuits, and with light doughs such as cinnamon bread and brioche. Crunchy and buttery textures create unique combinations, and the science of food pairing has huge potential here. It also pairs with milk, creams and foams, and with toast with cheese and gorgonzola, cheese-and-strawberry toast, and breads or bases with cream cheese and requeijão.

    A different world from hot coffee

    Cold extraction leaves behind part of what hot water pulls out, mainly the oils. That is why it is another world. What guided me was noticing that my body behaves differently with hot extraction, with the fat and the compounds dissolved by heat. There must be a field of research behind this that I chose not to dig into; I simply find that cold brew gives me comfort, clean energy and flavour, with no hangover, indigestion or crash. Hot coffee has all its wonder, uniqueness, habit, affection and the history I have with it. But direct methods, such as metal filters and espresso, leave me with some discomfort afterwards; paper-filtered methods, especially the Chemex, sit better with me. This is my experience: watch your own.

    The next leap: water

    I have been talking with Thiago Motta, and I see great potential to unlock all the know-how on water and its sensory interaction in this brew. I think the big leap ahead is water calibration for cold brew.

    Why I love cold brew

    Cold brew has far more potential for complex flavour variation than hot-brewed coffee. You can play with time, grind and temperature and pull different sensory fractions with every batch.

    And the energy is different. In my experience, cold-extracted caffeine feels clean: no anxiety, no jitters, no fog. A journey of its own.

    There is no single right cold brew

    A survey of 125 people who make cold brew, at home and professionally, found everything: extractions from under 8 h to over 44 h, from 0 °C up to room temperature, from under 50 to over 200 g of coffee per litre, and grinds from fine to coarse. The authors note that there is still no standardized process, nor a settled definition of what cold brew is (Claassen et al., 2021). In Brazil, the two experts interviewed in a UFRGS thesis say the same: preparation varies with origin, time, water and method, and what matters most is bean quality (Girardi, 2023).

    This page is my starting point, not the right answer. Change the time, the grind and the ratio, taste, and keep what you like.

    Calibrate your coffee

    Calibration Pad: a particle analyser on paper

    A sheet printed at exact scale and a phone photo are enough to measure your grind. That is how I measured the 953 µm of this recipe.

    Download to print at a photo lab (JPG 15 × 21 cm)Download PDF (print at home)

    How to print it at the right scale

    1. Download the JPG. It is already at the exact size: 1,772 × 2,480 pixels at 300 dpi, which is a 15 × 21 cm print.
    2. Take it to a photo shop or order it from an online photo lab. Ask for a 15 × 21 cm (about 6 × 8 in) print, preferably on matte paper (less glare in your photo).
    3. Ask for actual-size printing. Say it must print at 100%, with no fitting, no cropping and no "fill the page". Any enlargement changes the scale and ruins the measurement.
    4. At home, use the PDF and choose "Actual size" or "100%", turning off "Fit to page". Trim the sheet to 15 × 21 cm if your printer uses A4 or Letter.
    5. Check with a ruler. The bar below the circle must measure exactly 104 mm. If it measures 102 or 106 mm, the print was reduced or enlarged: have it reprinted.

    How to photograph

    1. Sprinkle a pinch of ground coffee inside the circle, with the particles not touching.
    2. Use soft light, from a window or an overcast sky, with no phone shadow on the paper.
    3. Shoot from directly above, phone parallel to the sheet, no digital zoom, with the whole circle and the 104 mm bar in frame.
    4. Upload the photo to an AI assistant and paste the prompt below. It works best with an AI that can run code to process the image.

    Prompt for the analysis

    You are a coffee grind particle-size analyst. I attached a photo of ground coffee spread over the Calibration Pad: a reference circle exactly 104 mm in diameter, a central crosshair with marks every 10 mm and, below it, a 104 mm bar.

Do the following, in order, running code to process the image if you can:
1. Find the 104 mm circle and the crosshair. Correct the perspective so the circle becomes a perfect circle and compute the scale in micrometres per pixel from it. Check the scale against the 104 mm bar.
2. Correct lighting and shadows and separate the particles from the white background. Split touching particles and ignore the printed lines of the pad.
3. Measure each particle by its equivalent diameter (the diameter of a circle with the same area).
4. Exclude fines below 300 µm and obvious clumps (very irregular shapes or ones much larger than the rest). Say how many you excluded.
5. Report the number of particles measured, the mean in µm, the median (D50), D10, D90 and the span.
6. Draw a histogram on a logarithmic scale from 0.3 to 3.0 mm, with the mean marked.
7. Say which brew method this grind corresponds to (espresso, filter, French press, cold brew) and whether it suits cold brew (reference: about 800 to 1,200 µm).

If the photo does not allow a reliable analysis (circle cut off, strong shadow, overlapping particles), tell me what to fix before I shoot again.

    Accuracy depends on the photo and the AI used. To compare grinds, always use the same pad, the same light and the same AI.

      Lecture calculations, redone
      • Hardness from a label: Ca 8.8 mg/L × 2.5 = 22; Mg 4 mg/L × 4.1 = 16.4; total 38.4 ppm CaCO₃ (below the SCA 50).
      • Alkalinity: bicarbonate 50.71 mg/L × 0.8 = 40.5 ppm CaCO₃, right on target.
      • Why espresso tastes more acidic: at 40 mg/L alkalinity, 30 mL of espresso carries 1.2 mg of buffer and 150 mL of filter carries 6.0 mg, for the same 10 g of coffee.
      • Same sum for our 1:10 (my calculation): 800 mL carries 32 mg of buffer for 80 g, or 0.4 mg per gram. That sits between espresso (0.12 mg/g) and filter (0.6 mg/g): alkalinity weighs less here than in a filter brew.
      Observed in the lecture
      • Lemon-water test: a water with 365 mg/L bicarbonate (292 ppm) kills the acidity; another with 22 mg/L (16 ppm) leaves it intact.
      • Waters vary a lot across Brazil; with low minerals, many cup at 9–10 g per 150 mL (1:16.6 to 1:15) instead of the 1:18 protocol (8.25 g per 150 g).
      • With higher-alkalinity water, a lower ratio (more coffee) is recommended to bring acidity forward.
      • The lecture mentions the anomalous behaviour of water at 4 °C in cold brew, without developing it.
      For your own study
      • Read the label of your mineral water and do the two sums above. Target: hardness 50–175 and alkalinity near 40.
      • Simple experiment: same coffee and recipe in two waters (one low, one high bicarbonate). Taste blind and note acidity, sweetness and finish.
      • A question for Thiago: in cold extraction, does magnesium still bring out floral notes as it does hot?

    2. HENDON, C. H.; COLONNA-DASHWOOD, L.; COLONNA-DASHWOOD, M. The role of dissolved cations in coffee extraction. J. Agric. Food Chem., 62(21), 4947–4950, 2014. open

      Qualitative reading of the energy chart in the paper: "strong" is the highest of the set, "≈ water" is comparable to the ion–water bond. Exact values are in Figure 3 of the paper.

      What was observed
      • Binding energy grows with ion charge and falls with radius: Mg²⁺ (distance 1.92–2.00 Å) binds more than Ca²⁺ (2.15–2.23 Å) and far more than Na⁺ (2.24–2.28 Å).
      • Sodium hardly helps: it binds acids about as water does and binds caffeine and eugenol less than water. Softener water (sodium-rich) prevents scale but does not extract better.
      • Magnesium also pulls quinic and chlorogenic acids, which the authors find unpleasant. That is where bicarbonate comes in: it neutralises chlorogenic acid (pKa 2.66) more than the small acids (pKa 3.14–3.86).
      • No single water suits every coffee. And each coffee is roasted for the roaster's water: the more extracting the water, the lighter the roast tends to be.
      Limits
      • It is computation, not measurement: gas phase, no explicit water. With a solvent model energies drop about 30%, keeping the order.
      • It does not measure taste or cup concentration. The experimental test came later (see Bratthäll below) and did not confirm the extraction effect.
      For your own study
      • Keep the order Mg > Ca > Na as a working hypothesis, not a measured fact.
      • Does it hold at any temperature? The order of energies does; extraction speed in the cold is another matter the paper does not address.

      What was observed
      • Perceived acidity follows titratable acidity, not pH. Because alkalinity reacts with the extracted acids, it directly reduces what is perceived.
      • The water:coffee ratio changes everything: espresso 1:2, filter 1:15 (60 g per 1,000 g of water gives about 880 g of beverage). The buffer weighs far less in espresso.
      • A water "TDS meter" is a conductivity meter: on its own it tells neither hardness nor alkalinity.
      • There is probably no single optimum for hardness and alkalinity: it depends on green coffee, roast, method and audience.
      For your own study
      • Our 1:10 is closer to filter than to espresso: water alkalinity still matters, just a little less.
      • A titration kit (hardness and alkalinity) is worth more than a TDS meter for comparing mineral waters.

    What science confirms and what is my reading

    • Confirmed: coarse grind with less time gives the sweetest, most fruity and floral drink; more time raises solids and titratable acidity (Cordoba 2019).
    • Confirmed: cold brew is more floral and less bitter and sour than hot brew (Batali 2022), with similar pH and fewer titratable acids (Rao and Fuller 2018).
    • Confirmed: caffeine reaches equilibrium in 6–7 h (Fuller and Rao 2017; Cândido 2023), so extra time changes flavour more than caffeine.
    • Confirmed: room temperature speeds up extraction; by 24 h solids match the fridge (Cândido 2023).
    • Confirmed: canephora brings more bitterness and astringency (Portela 2020); darker roast reduces floral and fruity notes (Shi 2024).
    • My observation: the floral, then nuts, then chocolate order along extraction, the weights of each radar axis, the effect of ratio on the map and the pre-calibrated recipe points. They are consistent with the studies but were not measured: taste and adjust.
    • Open: what is listed above as my observation is perception from observation, not yet scientifically validated. No cold brew study with medium-roast Brazilian arabica measures these notes one by one, nor is there one on water calibration for cold brew.
